PS-06 · Supply Chain Resilience Co-Pilot · Stage 1: understand and scope · synthetic data (Veltrix Components, fictional)

Veltrix saw most of its disruptions coming. It didn't act on the warnings.

In 24 months, 18 disruptions cost ₹170 crore of revenue. 15 of 18 had public warnings a median of 18 days before impact. The team noticed a median of 1 day after impact and decided after 9 days. 95.5% of the loss came through single-source parts whose stock ran out before supply came back.

3 · Alert definition

A signal becomes an alert only when all four are true:

  1. Linked. It names, or resolves to, a supplier, port or location, lane or chokepoint, or commodity in Veltrix's network with enough link confidence. If it can't be linked confidently, it goes to Needs review, never a guess. A dramatic headline about something we don't depend on is background noise.
  2. Credible and in horizon. It's a forecast or confirmed event, not commentary, and it falls within the 6-week likelihood horizon. Credibility comes from the source tier (official weather, port and trade bodies, then trade press, then general outlets) and from how many independent sources agree. Scope limits are respected: an advisory for the exercise zone only means low likelihood (T2).
  3. Material. The playbook impact method finds a part where effective delay exceeds days of cover, so revenue, margin or OTIF is at risk, and priority L × I ≥ 6. If cover is enough, it's logged as Watch: no action needed, with the working shown (T7). This alone would have saved most of the ₹4.57 cr of wasted spend.
  4. New. It isn't a repeat of an open event. A repeat attaches to the existing alert as another source (T3).

Every alert contains: event summary and all cited sources · linked suppliers, ports, lanes, parts, SKUs and customers, each with link confidence · exposure table (part, cover, effective delay, shortage days, binding part per SKU) · units short, revenue and margin at risk, customer OTIF vs 95% · likelihood, impact and priority with a one-line reason each, plus overall confidence · response SLA from the playbook (log, 2 days, 24 h, war-room) · tag: warning-led or sudden · ranked options with cost, time to effect, net value and required approver (never shown as approved) · owner (category manager for the part's category) and status.

Alert budget: only priority ≥ 6 reaches the inbox. Everything else goes to the watch log, which is reviewed weekly. Alert fatigue is the first thing that kills tools like this.

5 · Answers from the problem-owner meeting

  • Which disruptions to focus on. Repeatable, frequent and cross-geography ones. When looking for patterns, set aside outliers (over 3 months' delay or over $1M loss). Bucket everything as avoidable or non-avoidable. A supplier that has missed dates 10 times out of 10 is predictable, so supplier track record feeds likelihood. Design: avoidable/sudden tag; on-time rate and history raise likelihood.
  • What loss is acceptable. None. Count opportunity cost: idle teams, held-up downstream work and penalties, not just lost sales. Design: margin at risk + penalties + idle-line cost in net value.
  • How many alerts a week. It depends on the persona and company size. Ask how many they handle now, don't spam them, and attach a confidence score. Design: priority-≥6 inbox, confidence on every alert, per-persona views.
  • Noise vs credibility. Roughly 60–70% of world news is irrelevant, and some of it is TRP-driven noise. Verify by pattern, the way plagiarism checkers do or the way WhatsApp flags heavily forwarded content. Non-credible sources lose government or ministry clients instantly. Design: source tiers + corroboration count feed confidence.
  • Real time, predictive, actionable. Insight after the event is worthless. People are spread across time zones, and a morning hand-off loses two days. An alert has to say what to do, e.g. source from an unaffected country instead. Design: push to the owning category manager; ranked options on every alert.
  • Business angle. Who pays, at what price, on what model (subscription vs one-time)? Which personas: a small shop's supply chain vs a hyperscaler's, government vs private? Which stakeholders need to be involved (ports, forwarders)? You can't afford to scan everything. Design: start with a mid-size manufacturer, price on nodes monitored and signal volume, curate sources per tier.
  • Context. In a comparable real engagement for a large tech firm's data-centre build-out, losses were $2–3B a year. It took a year to diagnose: millions of rows bucketed into the top 15–20 reasons before any solutioning. Our Stage 1 is that diagnosis on Veltrix's data.
